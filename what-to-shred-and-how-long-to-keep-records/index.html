What to shred and how long to keep records

Last updated November 18, 2026

Shredding is permanent, so the first question is whether you still need the paper. This page summarizes two public sources. It is general information, not tax or legal advice. If a record matters for a dispute, a business, or an audit, ask a tax professional.

Tax records: what the IRS says

The IRS says you generally must keep records that support an item of income, deduction, or credit until the period of limitations for that return runs out. In short (IRS):

  • 3 years in most cases, counted from when you filed. Returns filed before the due date are treated as filed on the due date.
  • 6 years if you did not report income you should have reported, and it is more than 25% of the gross income on your return.
  • 7 years if you claim a loss from worthless securities or a bad-debt deduction.
  • Indefinitely if you did not file a return, or filed a fraudulent one.
  • At least 4 years for employment tax records, from when the tax is due or paid, whichever is later.

The IRS also says to keep copies of your filed returns, to keep property records until the limitations period expires for the year you dispose of the property, and to check whether insurers or creditors want records kept longer before you discard them.

Other papers: one state’s guidelines

The Washington State Attorney General’s office publishes retention suggestions. They are cautious, and they are one office’s guidance, not a national rule (What to Shred):

  • Tax records: seven years, “to be safe.”
  • Pay stubs: one year, then match to your W-2 and shred.
  • Bank statements: one year, except records tied to taxes, business expenses, home improvements, mortgage payments, and major purchases.
  • Credit card statements: at least 45 days, longer if needed for taxes or proof of purchase.
  • Medical records and bills: at least a year, often longer.
  • Insurance policies: the life of the policy plus five years.
  • Home purchase, sale, or improvement records: until six years after you sell.
  • Utility and phone bills: shred after paying, unless they contain tax-deductible expenses.
  • Warranties: keep while current. Expired ones can be recycled unless they contain personal information.

What is worth shredding

The same office suggests destroying papers that carry account numbers, birth dates, passwords or PINs, signatures, or Social Security numbers. Its list includes bank and credit card statements, canceled checks, pre-approved credit offers, pay stubs, tax forms, credit reports, expired IDs and passports, medical and insurance paperwork, and junk-mail address labels.
