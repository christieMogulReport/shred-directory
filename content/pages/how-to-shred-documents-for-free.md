You do not need a special business to get paper destroyed. There are three common routes, and the right one depends on how much paper you have and how much it matters that you watch it happen.

## 1. Free community shred events

Credit unions, banks, AARP chapters, city and county governments, and schools regularly host free, drive-through shred days. A typical one is run by a commercial shredding truck parked in a lot: you drive up, hand over your boxes, and the paper is shredded on site.

What to expect, based on events announced publicly:

- **Limits.** One recent announcement from a community center said, "Limit: 3 banker boxes/vehicle. Ends at noon or when full," and asked people to register ([post on X](https://x.com/Intl_District/status/2122392037458670078)). Another credit union's page said the paper is shredded on site and recycled ([Oklahoma Central Credit Union](https://www.oklahomacentral.creditunion/shred-day)).
- **Short windows.** Many run only three or four hours on a Saturday morning, so go early.
- **Sometimes members or residents only.** Check the host's rules. Some events are open to everyone and some are not.
- **Seasonal.** Many hosts run one or two per year, often in spring and fall, so a missed event can mean a long wait.

To find one, search for your city plus "shred day" or "shredding event," check your credit union and bank branch pages, your county or city solid-waste page, and the AARP events page linked below.

## 2. Drop-off shredding (pay by the pound)

Some shipping and office stores will take paper any day they are open. For example, The UPS Store location pages say they work with Iron Mountain: you weigh your documents, pay a per-pound price, and put them in a locked container ([The UPS Store, Tulsa, OK](https://locations.theupsstore.com/ok/tulsa/10026-a-s-mingo-road/shredding/)). FedEx Office says it offers in-store shredding at select locations, so call your store first.

Two things people are sometimes surprised by: the shredding usually happens later, not while you watch, and drop-off locations generally can't give you a certificate of destruction. Shred Nations says this on its Tulsa page, and advises mobile or offsite shredding if you need a witnessed shred or a certificate ([Shred Nations Tulsa](https://www.shrednations.com/tulsa/)). The same page says drop-off works best under about 3 boxes (roughly 90 pounds); above that, mobile or offsite service is usually cheaper. Prices vary by store.

## 3. A home shredder

For a small stack, a home shredder keeps the whole process in your hands. The Washington State Attorney General's office suggests a cross-cut model, and says to cut up expired credit and ID cards with scissors if your shredder can't handle plastic ([What to Shred](https://www.atg.wa.gov/what-shred)).

## Before you shred anything

Don't shred records you may still need. Read our guide to [what to shred and how long to keep records](/what-to-shred-and-how-long-to-keep-records/) first.

## Can you recycle shredded paper?

It depends on where you live. Cross-cut confetti has short fibers; the Washington State AG's page says it may go into yard-waste carts or compost, and that other sources say bundled shredded paper can be recycled, so check with your waste service provider ([source](https://www.atg.wa.gov/what-shred)).
